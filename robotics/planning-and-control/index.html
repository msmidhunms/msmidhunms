Robotics · Generative models

Planning and control in one network

People plan a task and control their muscles through one connected brain. This work brings the same idea to robots: a single generative network that turns a task into the joint motions that carry it out.

01 · Motivation

A child's first steps

A child masters walking within about two years. Touch, the sense of its own body and what it sees all feed back into the brain, which blends them into one picture and turns that picture into movement.

The child also does not start from nothing. Evolution gives it built-in biases, prior knowledge that makes walking quick to learn. This mix of sensory fusion and inborn understanding is the inspiration for building learning networks for robots.

Adults go further. People can learn many tasks, including very complicated ones, and learn them quickly. Behind every task is a close partnership between planning and motor control.

Sight

The retina collects light, and the visual cortex at the back of the brain processes it before passing it on.

Proprioception, the sixth sense

Receptors in muscles, tendons and joints report where each body part is and how it moves, without needing to look.

A map of the body

The parietal lobe gathers these signals and builds a spatial map of the body in relation to its surroundings. The cerebellum uses them for fine motor control.

03 · Plan and control

How people do a task, and the robot equivalent

The prefrontal cortex plans: it frames the goal and pictures the outcome. The motor cortex turns that plan into commands for muscles and joints. The cerebellum fine-tunes the movement as it happens. Vision and proprioception keep feeding back, so the plan adjusts while it runs. These regions sit in different places but are wired together, so planning and control work as one system.

04 · When control fails

A good plan is not enough

When motor control falters, movement breaks down even if the plan is fine. Alcohol slows and blurs the brain's motor commands, which shows up as an unsteady gait and poor balance. In Parkinson's disease, the loss of dopamine-producing neurons disrupts the signals that regulate movement, causing tremor, slowed movement and difficulty starting or finishing an action. In both cases the person may still know what to do, but the task is not completed, because the control fails.

05 · The approach

One generative network that plans and moves

If planning and control are joined in one connected system, as in the brain, the result can be both well planned and finely controlled. In a structured, static environment, where nothing moves unexpectedly, the body's own sense is enough. The network never receives proprioception as a separate input. It learns it internally: by training on joint commands, it builds its own sense of where the arm is and how it moves.

A task label stands for the goal, the way an intention does. Two conditional variational autoencoders were built, and both work in two phases:

  • Training. A recorded joint-command trajectory goes into the encoder, which compresses it into a latent code z. The decoder takes z and the task label and rebuilds the trajectory. The VAE loss, reconstruction error plus a KL term that keeps the latent space smooth, drives learning.
  • Inference. The encoder is no longer needed. A latent vector is sampled from the learned space, and the decoder turns it, with the task label, into new joint commands for the robot.

The two models differ in their layers:

  • Conditional CNN-VAE. The encoder and decoder are built from 1D convolution layers only. They read and write local patterns in the motion, like short movement primitives.
  • CNN-LSTM VAE. The encoder has n convolution layers followed by m LSTM layers. The decoder mirrors it, with LSTM layers followed by convolution layers. The LSTM layers add memory, so each part of the motion is shaped by what came before.
